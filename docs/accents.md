# Telling German accents apart: which words to listen to

Could the robot guess where a visitor comes from by a few spoken words? This page collects what a public
pronunciation atlas says about it: which words split the German-speaking area most cleanly, and which
small set of words would be enough for a rough guess.

**Nothing here is built.** It is a reading of the atlas, done on 18 November 2026, as groundwork.

---

## The source

The **[Atlas zur Aussprache des deutschen Gebrauchsstandards (AADG)](http://prowiki.ids-mannheim.de/bin/view/AADG/)**
of the Leibniz-Institut für Deutsche Sprache in Mannheim (Stefan Kleiner, with Ralf Knöbl, 2011 onward).

| | |
|---|---|
| Speakers | 671 pupils of about 17 to 20, plus 157 adults of 50 to 60 |
| Places | 196, in Germany, Austria, Switzerland, South Tyrol, Liechtenstein, Luxembourg and East Belgium |
| Recorded | 2006 to 2009: a word list of about 900 words, reading texts, a translation task, picture naming, an interview |
| A map | One coloured square per speaker, usually four per place, coloured by how that speaker said the word |

Each map comes with a commentary describing where each pronunciation is used. That is what was read
here: **all 166 map pages in the sections on vowels, consonants, unstressed syllables, further phenomena
and further results.** Every map was rated on two questions:

* **Does it draw regions?** From "two or three large connected areas with little mixing" down to "no
  regional pattern".
* **Can you hear it?** From "cannot be missed" (a different sound, a different stressed syllable) down
  to "a fine shade of a vowel".

The descriptions below are summaries in our own words. For the maps themselves and the exact wording,
follow the links to the atlas.

---

## What the atlas shows, in short

* **About one map in seven is both clean and easy to hear:** 24 of 166.
* **Consonants and word stress work best.** Vowel maps mostly show fine shades that are hard to hear,
  with a few exceptions.
* **Three things can be told apart well:** north against south in Germany, Switzerland against
  everything else, and Austria against everything else. Eastern Germany has a few markers of its own.
  Telling Swabia from Bavaria, or Hesse from the Rhineland, rests on one or two maps each.
* **No single word decides.** Every map has speakers who do not fit, and many speakers say the same
  word two ways.

## What marks each region

Read from the tables above. Each line is a tendency in the atlas, not a rule.

| Region | Listen for |
|---|---|
| **Northern Germany** | "König" with "-ich", "Chemie" with "ch", "Chips" with "sch", "Journalist" with "dsch", "Fabrik" with a long "ie", "Szene" with a plain "s" |
| **Eastern Germany** | "Balkon" and "Restaurant" ending in "-ong" and "-ang", "siebenter", "Chile" with "sch", "später" with "e", "Marzipan" stressed on the second syllable |
| **West and centre** | "König" with "-isch", "Chemie" with "sch", "Fabrik" with a short "i", "Chips" with "tsch" |
| **South-west (Swabia, Baden)** | "Chemie" with "k", "Baustil" and "Zahnbürste" with "scht", "ging" with a long "ie" |
| **Bavaria** | "Chemie" with "k", "König" with "-ik", "ging" with a short "i", "Kaffee" stressed at the end, often a rolled "r" |
| **Austria** | "Tabak" stressed on the second syllable, "Kaffee" at the end, "Verse" with "w", "später" with "e", "Balkon" ending in "-ohn", years read without "hundert" |
| **Switzerland** | "Motor" stressed on the second syllable, "Balkon" on the first, "Quark" with "ku-", "Chemie" with a rough "ch", a rolled "r", "Restaurant" ending in "-ant" |

## A set of twelve words

Enough to place a speaker in one of the seven regions above, if most of the words go the same way:

| Word | Decides mainly |
|---|---|
| König | north, centre or south |
| Chemie | north, centre, south, or Switzerland |
| Chips | northern half or southern half of Germany |
| Journalist | north or south |
| Fabrik | central and southern Germany, against the north, Austria and Switzerland |
| später | west and south, against north, east and Austria |
| Balkon | east and north ("-ong"), Austria ("-ohn"), Switzerland (stress on the first syllable) |
| Restaurant | eastern Germany, Switzerland |
| der siebte Juli | east or west |
| Tabak | Austria |
| Kaffee | Austria, Switzerland and Bavaria against the rest |
| Motor | Switzerland |

## What it would take to build

1. **A game the visitor chooses.** "Read these twelve words and I guess where you are from." Guessing
   someone's origin from their voice without asking is not something a robot in a public room should do.
2. **One small check per word,** deciding between two or three variants. The consonant checks are the
   easy ones: "k" or "ch" or "sch", "tsch" or "sch", "ng" or not. Which syllable is stressed is harder,
   and long against short vowels harder still.
3. **A simple count.** Each word votes for the regions that fit; the region with the most votes wins,
   and close calls are reported as such ("somewhere between Hesse and Saxony").

## Why to be careful with it

* **The atlas is near-standard German, read aloud.** Its speakers were pupils reading lists. Free speech
  shifts some lines: the southern "-ik" in "richtig", for example, shrinks to the southern edge once
  people talk freely.
* **People are not maps.** In the atlas, 97 speakers (14 %) said "richtig" two ways within one text.
  Many people have moved, or have parents from elsewhere.
* **It is fifteen to twenty years old,** and the speakers were young then. Several maps show changes in
  progress between the pupils and the older group.
* **The ratings are ours.** They come from the written commentaries, as summarised by a tool. The map
  pictures themselves were not looked at one by one, and nobody checked the ratings a second time.
* **The recordings cannot be used.** The atlas allows downloading its sound clips only as examples for
  school and university teaching. Research use needs the institute's permission, and commercial use is
  not allowed. Building on the published findings is one thing; training software on the clips is another
  and would need to be asked for.
